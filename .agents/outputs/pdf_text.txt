INTRODUCTION
The first signs of the Aetherfall are not armies or falling stars. They
are smaller: a healing prayer that falters, a flock that refuses to land, a
cup of water that tastes faintly of ash. This volume begins in the
road-village of Redhold, where strangers are forced together by
violence, and carries them through Hallowmere Abbey to the
fortified capital of Calladris.
The story should feel like the opening of a long descent. The world is
still recognizable, but its promises have begun to fail.
RUNNING THE ADVENTURE
This adventure is written for four to five 1st-level characters. They
should reach 2nd level after surviving the attack on Redhold, 3rd
after resolving the Abbot’s Trail, and 4th after earning meaningful
standing in Calladris. The volume ends before the hidden ruler of
Calladris is confronted.
The central rhythm alternates between urgent danger and uneasy
stillness. Redhold is immediate survival. The road is dread and
evidence. Hallowmere is fragile sanctuary. Calladris is order that has
become suspiciously perfect. Avoid explaining the Aetherfall too
early. Let the characters collect symptoms before they are given
names.
What the Characters Know
The characters do not begin with the words Aetherfall, Executor of
Grace, or Sepulcher of Silence. Most people call the spreading
disaster the Blight, the Sickness, the Ash, or simply the trouble in
the east. Even learned clergy disagree over whether it is plague,
curse, failed magic, divine judgment, or war.
THE YEAR
The campaign opens in 3000 AoM. The Weaver fell a thousand years
ago; the blight reached the eastern marches of Siorlas four centuries
back. Nobody alive remembers a world that worked. What is new is
not the wound but its arrival here, at the western edge, where
Valedorn has spent generations assuming the trouble belonged to
someone else.
ADVENTURE OVERVIEW
1. Redhold. The characters begin separately in a small waystation
village. Mist gathers. Goblins erupt from the road and fields, first
in panic and then under a shared sigil. The alderman’s son takes
command of the defense, and by dawn the village has a debt it
cannot pay.
2. The Road to Hallowmere. Four travel legs reveal scarcity,
broken magic, refugees, and creatures fleeing something worse.
3. Hallowmere Abbey. The monks feel the same spiritual weight
as the characters, but their abbot has been missing for three nights.
He left with a royal envoy bound for Calladris.
4. The Abbot’s Trail. Evidence at the abbey and along the road
reveals that the abbot reached the capital, sought an audience, and
vanished. His abandoned mission becomes the party’s charge.
5. Calladris. The party arrives during the unveiling of a royal
commemorative statue. Many adventurers seek answers. Sideron,
right hand of the absent king, directs petitioners into useful
errands while the city’s deeper horror remains hidden.

BROKEN MAGIC
Magic in Aetherfall is not weaker. It is unreliable. Since the Fall of
the Weaver, every power that reaches into the world must cross a
wounded medium to arrive. This applies to all of it — arcane study,
divine prayer, patron pact, primal covenant, innate gift, bardic art. A
cleric’s god still answers. The answer has to travel the same broken
road as everything else.
Nothing about a caster’s faith, training, or bond has failed. The world
between them and their power has.
The Spellcasting Check
When you cast a spell, roll d20 + spellcasting ability modifier +
proficiency bonus. The DC is 11 + the spell’s level; cantrips use DC
11.
On a success the spell is cast normally. On a failure the spell fizzles.
The action is spent. The magic gutters — sparks at the fingertips, a
word that will not finish, a holy symbol that stays cold. The slot is
not expended. Nothing was cast, so nothing was spent.
Attack Spells: One Roll, Two Numbers
A spell requiring an attack roll would otherwise be gated twice. It is
not. Because the Spellcasting Check and the spell attack roll use
identical modifiers, they are the same roll. Roll once and compare
the total to both the DC and the target’s AC.
• Beats both. The spell forms and hits.
• Beats the DC, misses the AC. It forms and goes wide. An
ordinary miss.
• Fails the DC. It fizzles in your hand. Nothing leaves you; no
attack is made, and nothing can be counterspelled.
This produces the setting’s signature texture automatically. At low
levels the AC is the harder number, so cantrips rarely fizzle — they
simply miss. At high levels the DC dwarfs any armor. Small spells
miss. Great spells fizzle.

What Does Not Require a Check
No check: magic items, including items that cast spells — a made
thing holds its own pattern and does not reach through the wound to
find it. Also potions, consumables, and class features that are not
spells (Channel Divinity, Lay on Hands, Bardic Inspiration, Ki,
Rage, invocations, Divine Smite).
Check required: all spellcasting from every source, and spell
scrolls — a scroll is a spell waiting to be cast, not a finished effect.
A failed scroll is not consumed.
Magic items are therefore worth far more here than in ordinary play.
That is intended.

Do Not Roll for Every Spell
The earliest sessions should establish dread, not punish a player for
choosing a caster. Call for checks only inside marked unstable
zones, during surges of ash-mist, or when a scene specifically says
the Weave is under strain. This volume uses checks sparingly so the
first failures feel significant rather than routine.
Misfires: The Natural 1
House rule, all rolls except death saves: a natural 1 succeeds —
and costs. The roll beats every number it needed. The spell forms.
The attack lands. And something tears. Resolve the effect fully, then
apply a cost. It lands after the success, never instead of it.

For martial misfires, prefer a cumulative −1 to a weapon until a smith

THE ASH-MARK
Necromantic corruption and the blight share one track. Twelve stages
climb from the fingertips through hand, forearm, elbow, shoulder,
and chest to an enthrallment threshold. A fighter clawed in the first
session and a warlock who dabbles in death magic are walking the
same road. The blight does not care how you arrived.
Optional — Pushing Through. When a caster fails a Spellcasting
Check, they may force the spell through anyway. It succeeds, and
they advance one stage on the Ash-Mark Track. This converts the
rule from a tax into a temptation. Every failure becomes an offer.
HORROR WITHOUT
HELPLESSNESS
The campaign deals with disease, spiritual corruption, living
petrification, loss of memory, and hidden civic violence. Describe
consequences clearly, but preserve meaningful choices. A clue
should never require a single successful check. Failure changes the
cost, timing, or danger; it does not end the story.
RECURRING CLUES
• The Eye Sigil. A crude eye scored through with a vertical line
appears on goblin shields, road markers, confiscation orders, and
eventually in the geometry of Sideron’s civic seals. Nobody in
Valedorn can read it.
• Serene Faces. Victims are found without the fear expected of
violent death. They look relieved, emptied, or quietly accepting.
• Untouched Calladris. Crops, streets, lamps, and fountains in the
capital appear healthier than those outside it. The perfection is
maintained, not natural.
• Missing Authority. Orders are always issued in the king’s name,
but the king is never seen. Every delay is reasonable when
considered alone.
• Old Graces. Hallowmere preserves broken references to Keepers,
Graces, and judges beneath the Veil. The terms should not yet be
explained.

CHAPTER ONE
REDHOLD
The village that becomes a battlefield
Redhold is a small road-village whose value lies in ordinary things: a
warm room, a repaired wheel, a dry loft, a bowl of stew, and news
exchanged beside the fire. The campaign begins by letting the
characters briefly inhabit that ordinary life. Then the mist arrives.
REDHOLD AT A GLANCE
Redhold stands where a maintained crown road narrows between
grain fields and a low wooded ridge. It is not walled. Fences,
drainage ditches, stone foundations, and close-built yards provide the
only defensible lines. Its people are accustomed to travelers and can
usually tell the difference between a merchant, a pilgrim, a deserter,
and a thief within a few exchanged words.

KEY LOCATIONS
1. The Red Hart. The village inn and social heart. Its common
room can hold forty in discomfort and becomes the final refuge
during the attack. The cellar has two exits: stairs to the taproom
and a narrow delivery hatch behind the building.
2. Reeve’s Hall. A stout timber building with the village bell,
records, two locked chests of levy arms, and a loft used for
meetings.
3. Durn’s Smithy. A low stone forge. The quench trough, coal
dust, chains, hammers, and half-finished tools make the smithy
dangerous to attackers and defenders alike.
4. The South Barns. Grain, hay, and animals packed close
together. Fire here becomes an immediate threat to the entire
village.
5. The Pilgrim Well. A communal well beneath a small stone
canopy. The water tastes of iron on the morning of the attack.
6. The Holdt House. A long timber hall east of the well, older than
most of the village. Three generations live in it. Two of them will
not see the week out.
7. Eastern Verge. The crown road crosses a shallow ditch and
disappears into wheat and mist. The first goblins come from here,
fleeing rather than advancing.
THE HOUSE OF HOLDT
Read This First
The Holdt family is the emotional spine of the entire campaign, and
none of that weight should be visible tonight. Osric is a tired old
man. Garran is a competent neighbor. Liora is a child who waters
the horses. Play them as ordinary and let the campaign make them
matter.
Osric Holdt, alderman. Eighty-one years old, and the only man in
Redhold who remembers the last time a crown levy actually came
when it was called. He is stubborn, courteous, and slower than he
admits. During the attack he refuses to leave the Reeve’s Hall while
the records are still inside it. He survives the night. He does not
survive the week — see Aftermath.
Garran Holdt. Osric’s son, forty-four, twelve years a crown soldier
and eleven years home. He is broad, unhurried, and good in a crisis in
the specific way of a man who has already had his worst day. He
speaks of his daughter constantly and without embarrassment — in
the middle of a fight, over a meal, to strangers. Garran leaves
Redhold with the party. See the sidebar in Aftermath for running
him.
Liora Holdt. Garran’s daughter, eleven. She is at the pilgrim well
when the bell rings and she is not brave, clever, or marked in any
way. She is frightened and she does what she is told. If the party
saves her they will not think about it again for a decade.
OTHER PEOPLE OF REDHOLD
• Reeve Elswyn Marr. Direct, tired, unwilling to waste lives for
property. She judges people by what they do in the first minutes of
a crisis.
• Torren Vale, innkeeper. Broad-shouldered, calm, and excellent
at giving frightened people one task at a time. He knows every
cellar and alley in the village.
• Mara Durn, smith. A former caravan guard who refuses to
abandon her apprentice, even when the forge catches.
• Sister Anwen. A road-priest traveling to Hallowmere. Her
healing prayer fails once during the attack, leaving her shaken but
functional.
• Pell Reed. A twelve-year-old stable hand who sees the eye sigil
before anyone else and repeats a goblin’s terrified words: “The
eye brings death.”
• Hesta Vayle. A widow who keeps bees on the ridge and minds
other people’s children without being asked. Remember her name;
ten years from now the party will need to know who raised Liora.
STARTING THE CAMPAIGN
Place each character at a different location and ask what brought
them to Redhold. Good starting positions include the Red Hart, the
smithy, the pilgrim well, a rented loft, a stalled caravan, or the road
itself. Let each character complete one small social action before the
attack: order food, negotiate lodging, repair equipment, pray, gamble,

At least one character should exchange words with a Holdt — Osric
complaining about the road tax, Garran buying grain, Liora asking
whether their horse bites. Nothing more than that.
The afternoon has the dull silver color of rain that never quite
begins. Redhold smells of wet timber, horse sweat, forge
smoke, and bread cooling behind the inn. Travelers come and
go without ceremony. For a little while, the road seems like a
promise that the world still joins one place to another.
Then the eastern bell begins to ring. Not the measured toll for
fire. Not the quick double strike for riders. A child is pulling
the rope with both hands, screaming at something in the
fields.

MISSION STRUCTURE
The attack is not one continuous combat. Run it as a sequence of
short missions connected by movement through the village.
Characters may separate, but every mission should offer an urgent
reason to cross paths with another character. After two missions, the
party is recognized as the only group moving effectively through the
chaos.
MISSION 1: PANIC ON THE
EASTERN VERGE
Three small figures break from the wheat and tumble into the
road. Their skin is grey-green beneath streaks of soot. One
has thrown away its blade. Another clutches an arrow in its
side. The third turns, sees the mist behind them, and begins
hacking at the nearest living thing as if death in the village is
preferable to whatever follows.
Use three goblins, but begin one at half hit points and another without
a weapon. A character who speaks Goblin hears fragments: “Not
raid,” “eye behind us,” and “it makes the dead stand.” A DC 12
Wisdom (Insight) check confirms that the goblins are fleeing.
Capturing or calming one provides the first direct clue that the village
is not the true target.

A flaming arrow or burst of unstable magic ignites the south barns.
The characters have six rounds before the roof collapses. Each round,
one character can attempt one task; two successes secure the barn,

Environmental Weapons
A sack of flour or fine chaff can be thrown to create a 10-foot cloud
until the start of the thrower’s next turn. An open flame ignites it,

MISSION 3: THE BROKEN BELL
The Reeve’s Hall has been taken by sigil-bearing goblins. Their
leader has cut the bell rope and is searching the record chest for road
maps, census lists, and old royal seals. This is the first evidence that
the attackers are gathering information rather than merely stealing
food. One map has been marked with a line from Redhold to
Hallowmere.
Osric is here. He is standing between the goblins and the chest with
a boar spear he has not lifted in twenty years, and he will not move. If
the party does nothing he is badly hurt but survives. Garran arrives
one round after the party does, at a dead run, and the fight becomes
considerably easier.
MISSION 4: THE RED HART
HOLDOUT
The common room is no longer an inn. Tables stand on end
against the windows. The wounded lie beneath benches.
Someone has overturned a barrel of ale across the floor, and
every bootstep carries the smell of yeast and blood. Outside,
the village bell hangs silent. In the mist beyond the shutters,
something breathes with the wet patience of a bellows.

The final defense lasts five rounds. Each round, choose one pressure:
a window breach, fire at the rear wall, panicked civilians, a goblin
entering through the delivery hatch, or the shaman calling ash from a
corpse. The characters win by holding until Torren opens the cellar
route and the reeve organizes a countercharge. They do not need to
kill every attacker.
Liora is in this room, under a bench, holding a stranger’s dog. Do not
draw attention to her.
THE ASHBORN EMERGENCE
When a creature dies in a patch of black residue, describe the ash
drawing inward against the wind. An ashborn creeper pulls itself
from the stain. This is the first unmistakably unnatural enemy. It does
not carry a sigil, speak, or retreat. It attacks the nearest warm creature
and dissolves into greasy soot when destroyed.
Encounter Caution
Four ashborn against four 1st-level characters is a genuine party
wipe. The raid should be mostly ordinary panicking goblins with
two or three ashborn among them. The party should be able to feel
the difference between the two without being told there is one.
AFTERMATH
At dawn the mist withdraws eastward. The village has survived, but
at least six buildings are burned and several farmsteads have gone
silent. The reeve cannot both protect Redhold and send a force east.
Hallowmere is the nearest place with healers, archives, and a
tradition of receiving strange reports.
Osric Holdt dies four days later. Not of a wound — of eighty-one
years and one night of running. He is buried on the ridge. It is an
ordinary death in a village that has just had several unordinary ones,
and it should be reported almost in passing.
Garran Joins the Party
Garran asks to come. His reasons are plain and sufficient: he has
soldiered, he knows the Calladris road, and his father just died
asking who was going to tell the crown. Hesta Vayle takes Liora.
Run him as a competent second, never a solution. He carries a
shield and opinions. He is worse than the party at everything except
keeping his head. He should never solve a puzzle, land a killing
blow on a boss, or know a piece of lore the party could have found.
He talks about Liora constantly. Once or twice a session,
unprompted, in the wrong moment. What she said about the goblins.
That she is afraid of the well. That she will be taller than him.
Players will start to find it funny. Let them. That is the machine
loading.
Garran dies at the end of Act I. Nobody sees it happen.
CLUES IN THE RUINS
• The eye sigil is painted in lampblack mixed with a metallic grey
powder that does not wash away normally.
• The organized attackers carried lists of wells, granaries, and road
distances. The marks beside Redhold are complete; Hallowmere is
circled.
• A dead shaman’s focus contains a bead of black glass that is warm
despite the cold morning.
• A survivor saw one armored figure standing motionless in the
mist while goblins ran around it. No footprints remain where it
stood.
• Sister Anwen’s healing failed inside the mist but worked after
dawn, though weakly.
Why Redhold?
DM only. A waystation of no strategic value was attacked by a force
that came carrying survey lists. The party will never ask why
tonight, and they should not be able to answer it for two acts.
The answer is that Redhold sits on ground the sickness has never
rooted in, and something that can read prophecy wanted that ground
spoiled before it could be used. Six strangers who happened to be
standing there stopped it without knowing what they had done.
REDHOLD REWARDS
The village can provide ordinary equipment worth up to 20 gp per
character, two potions of healing from the reeve’s levy chest, and
letters naming the party as defenders of Redhold. More importantly,
the characters leave with people who know their names. That
memory should matter when Calladris later treats them as one more
band of petitioners.

HALLOWMERE
Four legs into a quieter dread
The journey from Redhold to Hallowmere normally takes three days.
In the present crisis it should fill three or four sessions — not because
every mile is mapped, but because each leg reveals a different failure
in the world. Travel until something matters; then let the encounter
become the map.

LEG 1: THE OPEN FIELDS
Redhold falls behind by degrees: first the chimneys, then the
bell frame, then the dark smudge of burned roofs. Ahead, the
crown road cuts through fields that should be green. The
wheat stands high but colorless, every head bowed in the
same direction though there is no wind.
The party encounters a wagon overturned beside a drainage ditch. A
merchant, two drovers, and a wounded crown courier are trapped
nearby while six goblins search the cargo. The goblins are not
wearing the eye sigil and flee if bloodied. They stole food because
the fields east of the grove have turned bitter.
The courier carries a sealed order for Hallowmere: “Prepare beds for
royal inspection and receive the king’s envoy.” The order is four
days old. He saw the envoy arrive at Hallowmere accompanied by an
older priest — the abbot — then depart east toward Calladris.
Encounter: The Weight in the Wheat
At dusk the bowed field begins to whisper. Characters who enter
must succeed on a DC 12 Wisdom saving throw or hear a familiar
voice asking them to lie down and rest. A character who fails is
charmed until the end of its next turn and uses its movement to walk
deeper into the crop. Two ashborn creepers wait in the center around
a dead scarecrow. Burning or cutting a path ends the effect.
LEG 2: THE GROVE ROAD
The road enters an old grove once maintained by crown foresters.
Distances become unreliable. A milestone marked Hallowmere – 9
miles appears twice. The second stone has fresh fingernail scratches
on its back.
The Doppelganger Campfire
The party sees a camp ahead occupied by travelers wearing similar
cloaks and carrying similar packs. From a distance they appear to be
the party themselves. Up close they are frightened refugees who have
unconsciously imitated the characters’ posture, phrases, and
arrangement. The mimicry ends when someone speaks a true name.
Nothing attacks; the scene exists to make the road feel porous.
The Skinwalker in the Mist
A corpse-thin humanoid follows at the edge of sight. It never runs
while watched. Use ghoul statistics, but it cannot paralyze; instead, a
creature hit by its claws cannot take reactions until the start of its next
turn. It retreats below half hit points, leaving footprints that become
those of a barefoot child.
Keeping the Stalker Frightening
Show the creature three times before initiative: reflected in a puddle,
standing behind the last traveler in marching order, and crossing the
road far ahead. Each sighting should be brief enough that the players
can doubt it, but concrete enough that caution feels justified.
LEG 3: THE DEEP WOOD
The road narrows beneath trees old enough to remember
other kingdoms. Moss covers the milestones. The afternoon
darkens without becoming evening. Somewhere ahead, a
prayer bell rings once and then stops in the middle of its own
note.
A ruined wayshrine stands beside the road. Its central icon has been
removed, leaving eight shallow niches and one larger empty

CHAPTER THREE
HALLOWMERE
ABBEY
A quiet house under a gathering weight
Hallowmere is holy, useful, and quietly tragic. Its people continue the
routines of hospitality even while healing fails more often and every
bed fills. The abbey is not under open attack. Its danger is absence:
the one person expected to understand the signs has gone missing.
The abbey rises from the low country without grandeur: grey
walls, steep roofs, a square bell tower, and gardens enclosed
against the wet wind. Lamplight glows behind narrow
windows. At the gate, a young monk looks first at your
weapons, then at the wounded, and finally at the road behind
you.
“You came from Redhold,” he says. It is not a question.
“Then you feel it too.”

THE WEIGHT
Every member of the abbey feels a heaviness behind the eyes and
sternum, strongest during prayer. It is not disease and cannot be
detected as poison. Characters who spend an hour in silent devotion
may make a DC 12 Wisdom (Religion) check. On a success they
sense not an attacking presence but a pressure toward stillness: the
temptation to stop asking, stop fearing, and accept that everything is
already resolved.
A False Comfort
Sideron’s influence reaches Hallowmere only as an echo. It cannot
petrify anyone here, but it suppresses urgency. NPCs repeatedly
suggest waiting one more day, trusting the crown, or accepting that
the abbot chose not to return. The party’s role is to keep concern
alive without turning every monk into an enemy.
Garran is immune to this in a useful way. He is not devout and the
pressure reads to him as ordinary institutional cowardice. He will
say so, out loud, at the wrong moment. Let him.

cannot cure a scratch a goblin gave them four days ago.

CHAPTER FOUR
THE ABBOT’S
TRAIL
Search, reconstruction, and a charge left unfinished
Abbot Caedmon did not vanish in the countryside. He accepted a
royal summons, reached Calladris, and disappeared within the
machinery of the capital. The characters’ first task is to prove that
fact. Their second is to understand what he was trying to do.
BEGINNING THE SEARCH
Prior Meriel grants the party access to the abbot’s lodging and
archive if they present evidence from Redhold or agree to carry word
to the crown. The search should combine physical clues, interviews,
and one short encounter. No single failed roll prevents success.
The Abbot’s Lodging
The room is orderly. Travel boots are missing. A spare cloak
remains. The writing desk holds three drafts of a petition, a rubbing
of an old inscription, and a list of questions rather than conclusions.
The petition’s final line reads: “If peace has truly been preserved in
the capital, then its manner of preservation must be known.”
The Sealed Cabinet
The silver key from the ruined wayshrine opens it. Eight shelf
spaces are marked with simple virtues: Humility, Consolation,
Meekness, Hunger, Mercy, Purity, Peace, and Witness. Only
fragments remain. The Peace shelf is empty except for stone dust
caught in the wood grain.
A DC 12 Intelligence (Investigation) check finds that the dust is
marble, not plaster. A DC 14 Intelligence (Religion) check identifies
the arrangement as a pre-modern judgment cycle rather than a current
devotional practice.
Witnesses
• Novice Talm. The envoy introduced himself as Ser Joric Vale but
wore no travel dust and never removed his gloves. He carried a
royal seal with an eye-shaped flaw in the wax.
• The Gate Porter. The abbot, envoy, and two mounted guards
departed before dawn. The guards returned alone near midnight,
saying the abbot continued with a faster patrol. Neither guard
entered the abbey.
• Orchard Worker. Found a strip of the abbot’s satchel leather
caught on the east gate, cut cleanly rather than torn.
ENCOUNTER: THE RETURNED
MULE
Near the burial wall, something strikes the outer gate once,
waits, and strikes again. A riderless mule stands in the rain.
Its reins are cut. Its flanks are grey with road dust, and tied

Do not identify Sideron yet. Hallowmere’s records describe offices,

CHAPTER SIX
CALLADRIS
The great coastal crown of Men
Calladris is a port, capital, fortress, and symbol. It is also a city
whose order has been engineered to prevent anyone from looking too
closely. The party can enter, work, leave, and return without
immediately discovering the truth. Every quest below should reward
attention while preserving the possibility that the horror remains
hidden for several sessions.
Calladris rises in terraces above river and sea. The western
gates open beneath twin towers crowded with banners.
Canals carry clean water toward the harbor. Soldiers keep
lanes clear for wagons while clerks direct refugees into long,
patient lines. Bells mark the hour. Street sweepers move
behind the procession.
In Crown Square, a white cloth falls from a new statue of the
king. The crowd applauds exactly three times.
The statue depicts the king in ceremonial armor, one hand raised in
reassurance. It is an excellent likeness according to portraits, though
no one remembers the king sitting for the unknown sculptor. Sideron
stands beneath the plinth and speaks of peace, continuity, and the
crown’s unbroken attention. The king does not attend his own
commemoration.

THE WEARY CROWN
This large, unfashionable inn serves as a base for petitioners,
adventurers, provincial officials, and people waiting for decisions
that never arrive. The proprietor, Jessa Venn, keeps a slate listing
which guests have been summoned to the palace. Names sometimes

SIDERON, RIGHT HAND OF
THE CROWN
Sideron appears as a severe, cultivated royal advisor in middle age.
He speaks softly, never wastes a word, and praises useful action over
accusation. He does not threaten the party. He gives them exactly the
sort of work heroes should want: rescue, investigation, mediation,
and protection. Every task also removes a problem from the city or
teaches him how the party behaves.
• He calls coercion relief from disorder.
• He never says the king is ill; he says the king is preserving his
strength.
• He answers direct questions with a duty the party can perform to
earn a fuller answer.
• He regards public fear as a contagious civic wound.
• He is genuinely pleased when the party prevents violence, even if
they expose one of his lesser servants.
The Hidden Truth
Sideron is the fallen Keeper of Peace, one of the Executors of Grace.
His Sepulcher of Silence turns living beings into conscious stone.
He has removed the ruler from public life, controls the royal council
through fear and dependency, and uses the city’s night systems to
erase visible evidence of the Blight.
The main text never requires the party to learn all of this during
the opening volume. They should leave with a face, a name, and no
framework.
THE ROYAL COUNCIL
• Lady Chancellor Marwen Hale. Believes order must be
preserved until the king recovers. She notices legal inconsistencies
but fears a succession crisis.
• Harbor Marshal Tovin Rell. Practical, loyal to the city,
increasingly suspicious of night removals from the docks.
• High Prelate Osric Dane. Publicly teaches that Calladris is
preserved by faith. Privately his strongest prayers fail inside the
palace.
• Guild Speaker Ilya Marr. Wants the roads restored and will fund
adventurers who solve practical problems without embarrassing
the crown.

THE SEVEN CALLADRIS
QUESTS
The quests can be run in almost any order. The Silent Witnesses and
The Missing Abbot should occur before the first council hearing.
Completing four should reveal that the city’s perfect order depends
on removal and falsification. Completing all seven should make
Sideron aware that the party is no longer merely useful.
1. The Silent Witnesses
Hook. At dawn, a famous knight is found standing in a public
garden, turned to pale stone. His sword is lowered. His face is serene.
Locals insist that at last his anger left him.
DM Truth: The Two Manifestations
Sideron’s default is pacification, not terror. Those who never
resisted receive the gentle version, because silence only completes
what was already underway. The contorted faces belong to those
who spoke, fought, or warned.
The expression on the stone is a record of whether the victim
resisted. Every statue in Calladris is readable evidence. The serene
ones go into the plazas at dawn; the others go wherever the night
crews take what cannot be displayed. The city is not hiding
petrification. It is hiding the ones who fought.
The Garden Scene. The knight, Ser Cadoc Warrin, stands beside a
dry fountain. There are no signs of struggle. Stone dust forms a thin
trail toward a service alley.
The Night Route. Following the dust reveals a locked municipal
yard where covered stone figures wait for transport. A night
custodian and two stonebound peacekeepers guard the site. Not all
the shapes under the canvas are serene. One is screaming.
The Sculptor’s Mark. The base of Warrin’s boots bears a tiny eye
cut through by a vertical line — the same geometry as the Redhold
sigil, rendered with professional precision.
Reward. One Royal Seal, the support of Harbor Marshal Rell, and
the knight’s signet if he cannot be restored. Complication. The
Gilded Compass is offered the same contract, but instructed to treat
the figures as stolen civic property rather than people.
2. The Missing Abbot
Hook. Hallowmere’s letter is acknowledged, but palace clerks deny
that Abbot Caedmon ever arrived. A stable ledger, an innkeeper, and
a street preacher each remember him differently.
The False Ledger. The west gate register has been scraped and
rewritten. Heat or charcoal reveals the original entry: Caedmon, Ser
Joric Vale, two guards, three folio cases.
Ser Joric Vale is not a royal knight. No such name exists in the
current rolls — but the name appears in records decades apart, in
identical handwriting.
The Chapel of Saint Orra. A beggar saw a body carried here at
night. Beneath the altar, old stairs lead into a burial passage that
opens onto a municipal mud yard behind the Old City wall — the
place where the unshowable are stacked.
THE BOOK IN THE MUD
He is on his side in the muck, half under a tarpaulin, one arm
thrown out as though he were still reaching for something.
The rain has washed his face clean. His mouth is open. His
eyes are open. Whatever was done to him, it was not done
gently, and nobody arranged him afterward.
A foot from his hand, pressed into the mud and filled with
brown water, is a book.
Abbot Caedmon is stone, contorted, and discarded. He was not
hunted for the book — he was silenced the way everyone in Calladris
is silenced, for becoming agitated in public, and processed as routine.
Nobody catalogued what he was carrying, because nobody
catalogues the ones they hide. The most dangerous object in
Valedorn survived because the machine was not paying attention.
The Tome
On the cover, deeply cut: an eye, ringed by eight other symbols.
The same eye the party saw on a banner at Redhold. No name
accompanies any of them.
• The body text is in an ancient script. It is pre-mortal and sacred
rather than merely old. Translation magic returns nothing — not a
failure, but the blankness of a spell finding no language to
comprehend. It needs a scholar, not a spell.
• The marginalia are in the abbot’s hand, and readable. They are
working notes, not a finished translation: guesses,
cross-references, three questions for every answer.
• A large section of pages is missing, and was already missing
when he received it. This is why his notes are speculative. It also
means the torn section still exists somewhere.
• A frantic warning is scrawled across two pages and breaks off
mid-sentence.
• One margin note works a name out of the script and sets it
beside a symbol. The name nearly matches the man running this
city. Not exactly. Nearly.
• Another note guesses at Dunriael. Nothing concrete — just an
old man reasoning that the elves keep the longest records.
What the Ring of Eight Actually Is
DM only. It is a diagram of the true hierarchy, sitting in plain sight.
Players will read it as a pantheon, or as one god and eight servants.
Both readings are wrong in the specific way that matters, and they
will not know why until Act III.
On the near-miss name: the tome’s version is the true one. Sideron
is what three ages of drift did to it in human mouths. The party will
argue about whether the resemblance is coincidence. That argument
is correct — it is and it is not. They are carrying a true name and do
not know it.
On Dunriael: the abbot was wrong. He was a human abbot in
Valedorn working from memory and inference; he knew Siorlas was
old and knew where its capital was. That is exactly the mistake a
well-read outsider makes. Do not correct it here. Let them walk.

Hook. Guild Speaker Marr asks why wagons from the western royal

Hook. Brother Oren’s copied references point to a restricted royal

explanation, and leave Calladris for another objective. This is not
failure. The city is designed to be revisited — and it will be, twice
more, once outbound and once ten years later on the way home.
Every departure allows Sideron to adjust, reward rivals, alter
records, and make the next return more unsettling.
The revelation that the king is absent should feel discovered rather
than announced.

APPENDIX A
DM SECRETS AND
EXECUTOR LORE
What is true beneath the public story
This appendix is for the Dungeon Master. It explains the pertinent
Executor, the hidden condition of Calladris, and the fragments of
older theology that may be revealed without exposing the entire
campaign mythology.
THE HIDDEN STATE OF
CALLADRIS
Calladris appears clean because its corruption is gathered, redirected,
concealed, and embodied in people who cannot publicly testify. The
city is not immune to the Aetherfall. It is sacrificing truth to
preserve appearance.
• The ruler is not freely governing. The crowned sovereign has
been subjected to Sideron’s sentence and hidden beneath Crown
Hill.
• Sideron speaks in the ruler’s name. Some decrees are forged;
others were signed under influence. The council cannot verify the
source of royal orders.
• The elite guard is not wholly alive. Certain palace and night
units are denied rest. By day they stand motionless in sealed posts;
by night they carry out removals.
• Ash folk maintain the illusion. Laborers, custodians, and
controlled dependents clean streets, collect blighted matter, and
feed the city’s systems. Many are coerced; some believe the work
is necessary.
• The statue is a focus. The commemorative monument anchors
Sideron’s influence in the public heart of the city and acts as a
sympathetic link to the hidden ruler.
SIDERON THE
PEACEMAKER
First estate: minister of judgment, charged with stilling destructive
strife and confirming the peace that follows rightful mercy.
Fallen title: Keeper of Peace, Lord of the Sepulcher of Silence.
Public guise: royal advisor, right hand of the crown, architect of
civic continuity.
Corrupted doctrine: conflict ends when no one retains the power to
resist, accuse, warn, or grieve publicly.
The Law. Blessed are the peacemakers. In the first order the grace
sought reconciliation: the end of vengeance, the protection of the
innocent, and the difficult restoration of community after wrong.
The Subjugation. Sideron treats disagreement as the root of
suffering. To preserve peace he removes the capacity for public
disruption. Protest becomes illness. Warning becomes panic.
Resistance becomes contagion. The ideal citizen is not one who
freely consents, but one who no longer creates friction.
THE TWO
MANIFESTATIONS
This is the most important secret in the volume, and the easiest to
run wrong. Older records describe victims frozen in protest, fury, or
terror. Calladris finds them serene. Both are true.
Pacification is the default. Peace is his grace and he offers it first.
Those who never resisted receive the gentle version, because silence
only completes what was already underway in them. The contorted
faces belong to those who spoke, fought, or warned.
So the expression on the stone is a record of whether the victim
resisted, and every statue in the city is readable evidence. This is one
phenomenon plus a curation policy: the serene go into the plazas at
dawn, the others go wherever the night crews take what cannot be
displayed — cellars, the undercity, the lime yards outside the walls.
The Question Worth Leaving Open
Did the serene ones consent? If some accepted peace without
knowing its price, the party cannot cleanly call them victims — and
the ones who resisted become the only people in Calladris who were
fully alive.

The ring as it appears on the tome — eight about one
The centre is not one of the eight. It is the office they answered to,
and the party will not learn what that means for two acts. Everything
about the diagram invites the wrong reading, which is why it can sit
on a table in front of them for sixty sessions.
SIDERON’S THREE RULES
1. Keep power deniable. He uses decrees, contracts, queues, and
civic praise before supernatural force.
2. Turn conflict into administration. Every accusation becomes a
hearing, every witness a registration problem, every disappearance
a corrected record.
3. Remove proof before people. He prefers altered ledgers,
relocated statues, and compromised sponsors to public executions.
CLUES HE CANNOT ERASE
• Freshly petrified stone sweats clear water when exposed to
Hallowmere’s chapel lamp.
• The number of covered night wagons exceeds the city’s public
record of deaths.
• The same foreign mason’s mark appears beneath the jubilee statue
and on older hidden anchors beneath Crown Hill.
• Several royal ledgers repeat exact seven-day patterns, suggesting
copied lives rather than observed ones.
WHAT THE PARTY SHOULD
NOT LEARN YET
Do not reveal the complete heavenly history, the identity of the
centre of the ring, the infernal names of the Executors, or the
relationship between the Fall of the Weaver and the current
Aetherfall. The Calladris arc needs only one terrifying truth: an
ancient office of grace is present in the city and has made peace
into a weapon.
Reveal Pacing Across the Campaign
Act I. Something supernatural is happening in Calladris, someone in
the court is hiding it, and it has a name. A face, a name, and no
framework whatsoever.
Late Act II. The verse — that the name belongs to one of eight
fallen ministers, and that seven more are doing this elsewhere.
Early Act III. Full.
The risk is the thread going cold across the gap. Sideron must keep
reaching them: petrifications spreading beyond Calladris, the
abbot’s surviving lore surfacing in pieces, a recurring agent who
remembers their faces.

APPENDIX B
BESTIARY OF THE
ASHBORN
What the blight makes of things that were already alive
Ashborn are not a species. A creature that breathes enough ash,
drinks enough fouled water, or lives too long beneath a spreading
Dead Zone does not simply sicken and die. The ash takes root. It
fuses to hide and bone, crusts the joints, greys the eyes, and burns out
whatever the creature used to be — cunning first, then caution, then
finally the sense of self-preservation that would have sent it running.
What remains is driven. It moves toward living things and puts ash
into them.
Ashborn are not undead. They are alive, they can be healed, and
they can — very rarely, very early — be saved. Clerics find this
harder to bear than if they were simply corpses.
THE ASHBORN TEMPLATE
Apply to any base creature. This template is the authority; the blocks
that follow are worked examples.
• Constitution +4 (max 20), which supplies roughly +2 hit points
per Hit Die. AC +1 from ash-crusted hide.
• Gains darkvision 60 ft. if it lacked it, and immunity to
frightened.
• Loses ranged attacks and carried equipment, except weapons
fused into the hands. Ashborn do not draw bows. Creatures that
habitually wield weapons keep them — the ash grows around the
haft, and they cannot be disarmed.
• Gains bite and claw attacks at the creature’s existing attack
bonus, one die size below its primary weapon.
• Gains Ash-Mark, which replaces its Multiattack for the round.
One creature within reach that is below its hit point maximum
makes a Constitution save or gains the Blighted condition. No
attack roll.
• Gains Ashfall: on death it bursts into ash. Creatures within the
listed radius make a DC 11 Constitution save or are blinded until
the end of their next turn.
• Driven (CR 2 and lower only). Loses Nimble Escape, Surprise
Attack, Martial Advantage, and any stealth or tactical trait. Moves
toward the nearest perceivable creature and attacks.

The Driven Line Is the Design Centre
Below CR 2 the ash has burned out the mind — these things are
chaos. Above CR 2 they keep their traits, which means something in
the warband is still thinking. Put both on the same battlefield and the
party can see the hierarchy without being told about it.
THE BLIGHTED CONDITION
The mark occupies one stage on the Ash-Mark Track — the same
twelve stages as necromantic corruption.
• Your hit point maximum is reduced by the number of stages you
carry.
• At each dawn, make a DC 12 Constitution saving throw. On a
failure the mark advances one stage. Three consecutive successes
push it back one.
• At 0 stages you are no longer Blighted.
Removing It
• Lesser restoration removes one stage — and requires a
Spellcasting Check, which can fail.
• A week of tending at a house of healing removes one stage.
• Sanctified ground halts advancement without reversing it.
At low levels the party has no access to lesser restoration. A Redhold
infection can only be treated at Hallowmere, which is why they go
there — and why the abbey’s failing power matters so much when
they arrive.

THE SHAMANS
Ashborn shamans do not cast spells, and their powers require no
Spellcasting Check. This is deliberate and it is the most frightening
thing in the bestiary. The ash is not Weave magic — it is what is
replacing the Weave. So in a world where the party’s fire bolt gutters
and the cleric’s prayers arrive broken, the enemy’s power lands every
single time.
Do not explain this to the players. Let them notice.
None of the shaman powers are spells: they cannot be counterspelled,
they ignore region tiers, and they function normally in a Dead Zone.

TONE REMINDERS
• The world is not post-apocalyptic. Institutions still function,
which makes their compromises more consequential.
• Most NPCs are neither fools nor cultists. They are tired people
making locally reasonable choices under incomplete information.
• Use serenity as a horror image sparingly. The first statue should
be memorable; later victims deepen the pattern.
• Sideron is more frightening when he is helpful. He should solve
real problems while redefining freedom as disorder.
• The party is not chosen. They become important because they
keep acting when everyone else is encouraged to wait.
• Garran is not a hero and not comic relief. He is a neighbor who
came along, and he should be missed for ordinary reasons.
END OF OPENING VOLUME
The intended endpoint is a council hearing after the party earns four
seals, with the king still unseen and Sideron inviting the characters
into a more private relationship with the crown. The party should
know that Calladris is wrong, but not yet understand every
mechanism. They now possess allies, warrants, enemies, a book they
cannot read, and a direction that is confidently and completely
mistaken.
The world is wounded. The road still runs.
